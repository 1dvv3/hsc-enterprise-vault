Intelligent Systems — HSC Final Notes

✅ Named topic in the Trial (Task 4). 15 lessons across 5 weeks. Outcomes: EC-12-01, EC-12-02, EC-12-03, EC-12-04, EC-12-05, EC-12-06, EC-12-07, EC-12-08, EC-12-10, EC-12-11 Terminology: Terminology · Index: MOC

1. Decision Support Systems

A decision support system (DSS) is an interactive computer-based system that helps individuals and organisations make decisions by analysing large sets of data. It combines data, sophisticated analytical models and user-friendly software to support decision-making, especially in complex and uncertain environments.

1.1 The Three Categories of Decision-Making

Category Definition Human involvement
Structured Routine, repetitive decisions with clear rules and known outcomes None or minimal — the system decides and acts
Semi-structured Some elements can be automated, but judgement is still required The system recommends, a human decides
Unstructured Novel, complex decisions with no clear procedure. Depend on experience, intuition and negotiation The system informs discussion, humans decide

The tell in a scenario question: ask who makes the final call. Nobody → structured. System suggests, human confirms → semi-structured. Humans debating with data as one input → unstructured.

1.2 Worked Classification

Scenario Type Why
Supermarket automatically reorders stock below a threshold, no human involvement Structured Fixed rule, system acts alone
Factory shift scheduling follows strict rules, updates weekly without manager input Structured Rule-driven, no judgement
Airline route optimisation automatically updates routes before each flight Structured Calculation against known variables
Loan approval auto-approves or rejects standard applications; staff review only borderline cases Structured Rules decide; exceptions escalate
Financial advisor software suggests investments; the advisor makes the final decision Semi-structured System recommends, human decides
Medical diagnosis assistant suggests diagnoses; the doctor confirms or adjusts Semi-structured System supports professional judgement
Marketing dashboard shows feedback and trends; the manager uses experience and intuition Semi-structured Data informs, intuition decides
City transport planning — models, pollution data and public surveys discussed in meetings Unstructured No procedure; negotiation and competing priorities
University expansion — forecasts and financials plus community feedback and long-term goals Unstructured Novel, value-laden, no clear rule

1.3 Risks of Relying on a DSS

  • Over-reliance — users stop applying their own judgement
  • Garbage in, garbage out — poor data produces confidently wrong recommendations
  • Bias embedded in the data or rules is applied consistently and invisibly
  • Lack of transparency — users can't tell why a recommendation was made
  • Accountability gap — who is responsible when an automated decision causes harm?

2. Expert Systems

An expert system mimics the decision-making of a human expert in a specific domain using a knowledge base of facts and rules.

2.1 Applications

Diagnosis              medical, mechanical fault-finding
Monitoring             continuous observation against expected parameters
Process control        adjusting an industrial process in real time
Scheduling             allocating time, staff, machines
Planning               sequencing actions to reach a goal

2.2 The Three Key Features

Component Contains Function
Knowledge base Facts and rules Stores domain knowledge
Inference engine The rule base — a set of IF–THEN rules Applies judgement and reasoning to the knowledge base and the user's responses to reach a decision
User interface Input forms, output displays Allows the user to enter data and receive recommendations

Some systems add an explanation module (explains how the decision was made) and a data store (keeps input and results for later analysis).

2.3 Two Kinds of Knowledge

Source Nature
Factual knowledge Textbooks and shared resources, agreed upon by experts Objective, verifiable
Heuristic knowledge Experience and reasoning — "rules of thumb" Judgement-based, harder to codify

2.4 Rules and Boolean Operators

Rules take the form IF … THEN, combined with Boolean operators:

AND   &&     both conditions must be true
OR    ||     either condition may be true

IF (feathers && beak || 2 legs) THEN it is a bird

2.5 Forward vs Backward Chaining

Forward chaining Backward chaining
Also called Data-driven reasoning Goal-driven reasoning
Starts with Known facts / conditions A goal or hypothesis
Process Applies rules to derive new conclusions until it reaches a goal Works backward to check whether the facts support the goal
Best when You have data and want to know what it means You have a suspected answer and want to confirm it

Worked example — medical diagnosis

Knowledge base:

Rule 1:  IF fever AND cough        THEN might have the flu
Rule 2:  IF sore throat            THEN might have a cold
Rule 3:  IF body aches             THEN might have the flu
Rule 4:  IF might have the flu     THEN recommend rest and fluids

Forward chaining — known facts: fever, cough, body aches.

  1. Check which rules have matching conditions
  2. Rule 1 (fever AND cough) is true → conclude: might have the flu
  3. Rule 3 (body aches) also true → confirms the conclusion
  4. That conclusion triggers Rule 4 → recommend rest and fluids

Backward chaining — goal: does the patient have the flu?

  1. Find a rule that concludes "might have the flu" → Rule 1 or Rule 3
  2. Ask: does the patient have a fever? → yes
  3. Ask: does the patient have a cough? → yes
  4. Both true, so Rule 1 is satisfied → the goal is confirmed

Which is better? Forward chaining suits many inputs, unknown outcome (monitoring, sensors). Backward chaining suits few candidate outcomes, expensive data collection — it only asks the questions it needs.

3. Evolution of Expert Systems

3.1 Factors That Advanced Rule-Based Systems into Probabilistic Ones

Factor Effect
Processing power Enables analysis of vast datasets instantly
Data availability and access Digital records improve accuracy and coverage
Hardware costs Falling costs make powerful infrastructure affordable to deploy
Neural networks Allow interpretation with nuance and handling of uncertainty
Webometrics Measurement and analysis of web data ensures real-time updates refine decision-making

3.2 Changing Needs Driving Proliferation

Driver Meaning
IoT — Internet of Things Networked physical devices collecting continuous data
IoMe — Internet of Me Personalised, individual-centred connected services and data
Industry 4.0 The integration of automation, data exchange and intelligent systems into manufacturing and enterprise

3.3 The Progression

Stage What happens
Rule-based expert system Fixed IF–THEN rules, static knowledge base, no learning
Probabilistic expert system Adds probabilities to handle uncertainty (Bayesian networks)
Learning expert system Uses historical data to improve decisions (machine learning)
Fully intelligent system Integrates multiple inputs, adapts, predicts and may act autonomously

Example: MYCIN (1970s) diagnosed disease using fixed rules. Modern medical AI learns from millions of patient records, predicts likelihoods and updates its knowledge automatically.

3.4 Expert Systems vs Intelligent Systems

Expert system (rule-based) Intelligent system
Definition Mimics human experts using facts and IF–THEN rules Learns, adapts and reasons under uncertainty
Reasoning Deterministic — fixed rules only Probabilistic — handles incomplete data
Learning Limited — rules must be manually updated Machine learning improves decisions over time
Inputs Requires high-quality structured knowledge Integrates text, voice, images, IoT data
Example MYCIN Google Assistant, self-driving cars, recommendation engines

4. Intelligent Agents

4.1 Simplistic vs Complex

Feature Simplistic agent Complex agent
Definition Follows predefined rules or simple logic. Does not learn or adapt Uses machine learning, reasoning and adaptation to improve over time and predict outcomes
Behaviour Reactive — responds directly to specific conditions Proactive or adaptive — anticipates and adjusts
Knowledge base Small, fixed IF–THEN rule set Large and dynamic, updated through data and experience
Probability / learning None — purely deterministic Probabilistic reasoning, deep learning, reinforcement learning
Processing needs Low High — greater power and data access
Domain Simplistic Complex
Healthcare Rule-based diagnosis checklist IBM Watson Health using probability
Transport Traffic light timer Smart traffic system adapting to live flow
Home automation Fixed schedule thermostat Nest thermostat learning user habits
Finance Fixed budget calculator Predictive trading system
Customer service Scripted chatbot Conversational AI using NLP and intent recognition

4.2 Search Engine Agents

Predictive search strings — predictive text guesses the next word using natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning. You type "This weekend, I'm planning to" → it suggests "go", "visit", "relax".

Voice assistants — capture speech, convert it to text, interpret intent using NLP, then query and respond. More complex than text agents because they must handle accent, background noise and continuous speech.

5. Inference Engine Techniques

Inference engines are the core component of an expert system, responsible for drawing conclusions from data and rules.

Technique What it does
Truth maintenance system (TMS) Tracks which beliefs or facts are true in a dynamic environment. When new information is added or existing information revised, it consistently updates all related facts
Hypothetical reasoning Explores "what-if" scenarios — tests different hypotheses or assumptions to determine possible outcomes
Heuristic knowledge Uses practical "rules of thumb" based on experience rather than formal proof
Fuzzy logic Reasoning that is approximate rather than precise — handles uncertainty and imprecision. Values between true and false, not just 0 or 1
Ontology classification Organises knowledge into categories or concepts and defines the relationships between them, so the system can reason about data in a structured way

Confusing pair — Heuristic knowledge vs Fuzzy logic Heuristic = the rule is a shortcut from experience ("if it's cloudy in the morning, expect rain"). Fuzzy logic = the value itself is imprecise ("the room is somewhat warm" = 0.7 warm). Heuristic is about where the rule came from. Fuzzy is about how precisely it's expressed.

6. Hardware for Intelligent Systems

Hardware What it is Features Processing challenges Examples
Biometrics Measures human physical or behavioural characteristics for identification and authentication Unique to each individual · requires sensors or scanners · high accuracy but needs calibration and secure storage Encrypting sensitive personal data · matching accuracy in poor lighting or noise · ethical and privacy concerns Fingerprint smart locks · Face ID · biometric attendance
Haptics Uses touch, vibration or motion feedback to simulate physical interaction Tactile feedback via motors or actuators · both input (touch detection) and output (vibration/force) · enhances realism Real-time response with minimal delay · high bandwidth · hard to synchronise with visual and audio VR gloves in surgery simulators · force-feedback steering
Touch and gesture Sensors detecting physical contact or body movement Intuitive natural interface · multi-touch and gesture recognition · sensitive to environment Gesture recognition algorithms · misreads from movement noise · high processing for real-time recognition Industrial touchscreens · gesture-controlled kiosks
VR / AR Cameras, sensors and controllers track motion and overlay virtual content Immersive 3D interaction · motion and spatial tracking · high graphic demand Synchronising visuals and motion · high CPU/GPU load · latency affects experience AR-assisted surgery · VR maintenance trainers
Voice and sound Captures spoken commands or environmental sound via microphones Converts sound waves to digital data · requires NLP · keyword or continuous recognition Background noise · complex language understanding · accent and pronunciation variation Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant · call-centre systems
Microcontrollers Compact ICs performing specific control tasks in an embedded system Contains CPU, memory and I/O ports · real-time processing · cost-effective and energy efficient Limited memory and speed · requires efficient code · difficulty integrating multiple complex inputs Arduino in IoT devices · smart appliances
Sensors Detect and measure physical conditions and convert them to digital signals Converts real-world data into measurable values · analogue or digital · crucial for system awareness Calibration and accuracy · large volumes needing real-time processing · sensor drift or failure Temperature sensors · proximity sensors in robotics
Actuators and motors Convert electrical signals into physical motion Includes servo, stepper and DC motors · provides feedback via sensors Precise timing and calibration · energy and thermal management · synchronising with sensor feedback loops Robotic arms · smart windows · vehicle components

The system loop to memorise: Sensors collect → microcontroller processes against the knowledge base via the inference engine → actuators execute the decision. Sensors are input. Actuators are output. Microcontrollers are the control centre.

7. Computational Thinking in System Design

Strategy Definition Example Why it matters
Decomposition Breaking a complex problem into smaller, manageable parts Designing an autonomous car by separating object detection, route planning and speed control Smaller tasks are easier to analyse and solve, leading to more efficient development
Pattern recognition Identifying recurring patterns or trends in data or processes Facial recognition identifying the distance between eyes or nose shape Helps the system learn and predict more effectively
Abstraction Focusing on important details while ignoring irrelevant information A navigation app focuses on roads, traffic and directions, ignoring building colours Reduces unnecessary complexity
Algorithms Step-by-step instructions for solving a problem A store recommendation system suggesting products from user preferences Powers the decision-making process

Worked — an energy efficiency advisor expert system

Decomposition into subsystems:

Subsystem Description
User interface Collects user input and displays recommendations
Knowledge base Stores expert knowledge as rules and facts
Inference engine Applies logical reasoning to the knowledge base and user data
Explanation module Explains how the decision was made
Database / data store Keeps input and results for later analysis

Pattern recognition — recurring input patterns become rules:

Input pattern Recommendation
High daily energy use + poor insulation Improve insulation
High device count + long operation hours Switch to energy-efficient devices
Low natural light + high lighting energy Install skylights or LED lighting
Old appliances + frequent maintenance Upgrade appliances

Abstraction — keep what matters:

KEEP:    building type · energy consumption (kWh/day) · insulation quality
         device types and usage hours · lighting type
IGNORE:  wall colour · window size · furniture layout · device brand names

Algorithm:

START
  INPUT building_type, insulation, device_count, energy_use
  IF insulation = "poor" AND energy_use > 50
     THEN recommendation = "Improve insulation"
  ELSE IF device_count > 20 AND energy_use > 60
     THEN recommendation = "Replace old devices with efficient ones"
  ELSE IF lighting_type != "LED" AND energy_use > 40
     THEN recommendation = "Install LED lighting"
  ELSE
     recommendation = "Energy usage is optimal"
  OUTPUT recommendation
END

Modular design matters because new rules can be added later without rebuilding the system.

8. Communicating System Processes

Tool Shows Read
Flowchart Step-by-step logic in a process — the algorithm Top to bottom, left to right
Data flow diagram (DFD) How data moves through the system Follow the arrows
Infographic Simplifies complex processes for easy understanding. A static, single-point-in-time dashboard counts as an infographic Visually, at a glance

8.1 DFD Components

Component What it is Named with
External entities Sources or sinks of data — users, sensors Nouns (they are things)
Processes Calculations, logic — they change the data Verbs (they are actions)
Data stores Databases and files Nouns
Data flows Arrows showing data movement Labelled with the data

8.2 DFD Levels

Level Purpose Audience
Level 0 (context diagram) The whole system as ONE process, with its inputs, outputs and external entities Big picture for managers and clients
Level 1 Breaks the main process into sub-processes Helps programmers design modules and interfaces

Worked — SmartLoan Expert System

LEVEL 0
  Process:          Loan Decision System
  External entities: Bank Officer · Credit Bureau
  Data stores:       Applicant Database · Loan Rules Database

LEVEL 1
  1.1 Collect Applicant Data
  1.2 Retrieve Credit Report
  1.3 Apply Lending Rules
  1.4 Generate Decision Report
  Data stores: Applicant Database · Loan Rules Database · Decision Report Archive

Flowchart for the same system:

Start
  → Input applicant data
  → Check credit score      IF < 600 → Reject
  → Check income-to-loan ratio   IF > 40% → Review manually
  → Else → Approve
  → Display decision and reasoning
End

DFD vs flowchart — the distinction they examine: a DFD shows data movement; a flowchart shows decision logic. They complement each other, they don't substitute.

9. Disruptive Effects

Effect Explanation
Multitasking vs digital distraction Productivity apps and AI assistants aim to help users multitask, but frequent interruptions decrease attention span and lower work quality
Working differently to complete the same task Tasks become faster and more accurate but require new skills — giving clear voice commands, interpreting AI-generated results. Planning a trip traditionally means researching flights, hotels and activities separately; a travel AI automates it into a tailored itinerary
Automation of processes Repetitive tasks performed by systems, reducing human effort and error. Improves efficiency and lowers cost but displaces workers. Automated warehouses use AI robots to pick, pack and ship faster than humans
Impact of AI on employment Creates new jobs while eliminating others. Self-driving technology reduces demand for truck drivers but increases demand for AI engineers and vehicle technicians. Roles requiring creativity, emotional intelligence and complex decision-making are least likely to be replaced. Workers must reskill

9.1 Social vs Ethical Issues

Definition Concerned with
Social issues Challenges impacting individuals, communities or society when intelligent systems are developed and used Fairness, access, human rights, societal wellbeing
Ethical issues Concerns about what is morally right or wrong in designing and using systems Values — fairness, transparency, accountability, responsible use
Issue Category
Job displacement due to AI automation Social
Digital divide — unequal access to AI and technology Social
AI in healthcare — access to AI-driven medical care Social
Bias in AI algorithms Ethical
Privacy violations — data misuse and surveillance Ethical
Transparency of AI decisions Ethical
AI in law enforcement, e.g. facial recognition Ethical
AI weaponisation — military applications Ethical

The distinguishing question: does it concern who is affected and how (social), or whether it is right (ethical)? Job losses are social. The bias causing them is ethical.

10. Intelligent Networks and IoT

An intelligent network integrates devices and technologies to enhance operational efficiency, data management and decision-making. With IoT, it connects sensors, machines and servers for seamless communication and automation.

10.1 Infrastructure Requirements

Component Role
Networks with servers Central processing and coordination of device traffic
Local storage On-device or on-premises — fast, no internet dependency
Cloud storage Scalable, accessible anywhere, supports large IoT data streams
End-point devices The sensors, actuators and user devices at the network edge
Communication links Wired and wireless connections carrying data between endpoints, servers and cloud

10.2 Data in IoT Systems

Where data comes from: sensors, users, APIs, devices, social media, enterprise databases. Who provides it: machines (IoT devices) · humans (forms, voice commands) · external systems (cloud services, APIs). How it's collected: real-time (continuous streams) · batch (daily uploads) · manual entry.

How much to collect: enough for accurate decisions, but surplus data slows processing and wastes storage.

Type Example Use
Quantitative Temperature, heart rate, light intensity Measurable patterns
Qualitative Voice input, text feedback User experience insights
Structured Tables, spreadsheets, databases Easy to query
Unstructured Images, videos, text logs Requires more processing

10.3 Data Processing Stages

Stage What it does Example
Cleaning Remove duplicates, fix missing values, correct errors Remove duplicate temperature readings caused by a sensor glitch
Validation Check data meets specific rules and standards Ensure temperature readings fall within −20 °C to 60 °C
Filtering Select relevant data, discard surplus Keep the average daily temperature instead of every second's reading

Smart traffic system: IoT cameras collect thousands of images per hour. The system cleans (removes corrupted images), validates (checks timestamp and location tags) and filters (keeps only rush-hour data) before analysis.

10.4 Transmission

Flow type Description Example
Device-to-Device (D2D) Data exchanged directly between IoT devices Smart lights adjusting brightness from sensor input
Device-to-Cloud Sensors send data to the cloud for storage or analysis Fitness tracker sending heart-rate data
Cloud-to-Device Commands sent from cloud down to a device Cloud sends an alert to a phone app

Impact on network performance:

Structured data (small packets)      transmits FASTER
Unstructured data (images, video)    uses MORE BANDWIDTH
Surplus data (redundant readings)    causes LAG and HIGHER COST

Smart city network: filters air quality data locally at each sensor node, transmitting only hourly averages to the cloud — reducing network load while keeping insights fast and relevant.

12. Building Expert Systems

12.1 Rules, Facts and Decision Trees

A decision tree is a data model that helps a system make choices by breaking decisions into simple IF–THEN steps. Each branch is a question or condition (Is temperature > 30 °C?). Each leaf is an outcome or action (Turn on the cooling system).

Certainty factors attach a confidence value to a rule or conclusion, letting the system express how sure it is rather than a flat yes/no.

Where things live: the knowledge base holds facts. The inference engine holds and applies the rules. Both are examinable and frequently confused.

12.2 Verifying Data Sources

Verifying data sources is critical to ensuring a DSS provides reliable insight. Unverified data leads to harmful outcomes — a healthcare DSS acting on incorrect medication history risks dangerous drug interactions; a retail DSS on unverified sales forecasts causes excess inventory and holding costs.

Check Questions to ask
Source credibility Who collected it — government, university, private company? Is the source reputable? Is it biased (sponsored research, political influence)?
Accuracy and timeliness When was it collected? Is it still relevant? Any updates or revisions?
Compare with other sources Do other reputable sources report similar data? Where they differ, why?
Collection methods Surveys, sensors or user input? Scientifically sound and free from bias? Large and representative sample?
Transparency and documentation Is there a clear explanation of how data was collected? Are raw data and methodologies publicly available?
Bias or manipulation Could the data have been altered or misrepresented? Is there an agenda behind its collection or reporting?

13. Evaluating Intelligent Systems

13.1 How Expert Systems Contribute to Efficiency

Technology Contribution
Supercomputers Process huge datasets very quickly. Combined with expert systems, enable real-time analysis and adjustment — critical in financial markets and emergency response. The IBM Summit supercomputer uses AI-driven expert systems to optimise energy usage and support medical research
Digital assistants Provide hands-free, natural-language access to information and control
Autonomous vehicles Integrate sensor, map and traffic data to make continuous driving decisions
Streaming services Recommendation engines matching content to inferred preference

The efficiency arguments:

  • Analyse complex data in fields like climate science and genetics faster than traditional methods
  • Store specialised knowledge accessibly, allowing many scenarios to be evaluated in real time
  • Automate complex decision-making that would otherwise require substantial human expertise, reducing human involvement in supply chain management and predictive maintenance
  • Adjust recommendations continuously as new data arrives

13.2 Assessing DSS Output

Graph and compare proposed vs actual outputs. The gap between what the system predicted and what actually happened is the measure of its effectiveness.
