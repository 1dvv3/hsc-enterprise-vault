1. Ethical Issues in Data Use

The ethical use of data means ensuring fairness, accuracy, and respect for privacy and ownership.

1.1 The Seven Ethical Principles

Principle Definition
Consent Participants are informed about the purpose of the research, the data collected and how it will be used, and give informed consent
Confidentiality Researchers maintain the confidentiality of participants and their data unless otherwise agreed or required by law
Privacy Collection, storage and use of personal data respects individuals' privacy and complies with data protection laws
Data accuracy Data collected is accurate and reliable, minimising potential harm to participants
Data security Collected data is stored securely and protected against unauthorised access, loss or theft
Transparency Researchers are open about their methods, results and any potential biases
Responsibility Researchers take responsibility for the consequences of their research

Confusing pair — Confidentiality vs Privacy Privacy is the individual's right to control their personal information. Confidentiality is the researcher's obligation not to disclose it. Privacy belongs to the person. Confidentiality is a duty owed to them.

1.2 The Seven Ethical Issues

Issue What it means
Bias Systematic unfairness arising from how data is collected, processed or interpreted. Leads to discrimination. Addressed through diverse and representative datasets and awareness of prejudice in algorithms
Accuracy of collected data Inaccurate data produces incorrect conclusions. Addressed through regular validation, cleaning and cross-referencing with reliable sources
Metadata Data about other data — how, when and by whom it was collected. Proper management ensures transparency and improves data governance
Copyright and acknowledgement of source data Citing sources accurately and adhering to licensing agreements to avoid legal issues and credit original providers
Intellectual property and ICIP IP laws protect data creators. Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property recognises the right of Indigenous communities to control cultural heritage and traditional knowledge. Requires obtaining consent and acknowledging ownership
Permissions, rights and privacy of individuals Including cultural responsibility — handling data in ways that respect cultural protocols
Security Protecting data from unauthorised access, breaches and cyber attack

Confusing term — Metadata: data that describes other data. A photo's metadata includes the date, camera model and GPS location. Metadata is an ethical issue because it can identify people even when the underlying data is anonymised.

1.3 Software Features Affecting Privacy and Security

Feature Risks Mitigation
Autofill Data leakage if a device is lost or stolen · exploited by phishing to capture credentials on fake sites · unwanted sharing on shared or public devices · saves a record of everything entered Disable on public and shared browsers, review and delete saved data, use a password manager with encryption and two-factor authentication
Public or private connections Public Wi-Fi (airports, cafés, hotels) is generally unsecured — data can be intercepted. Private networks are more secure but can still be hacked or infected with malware Encryption via a VPN before transmission · firewalls to block unauthorised access · awareness of the risks of each connection type
Checkbox Users may inadvertently consent to data collection or third-party sharing without understanding the implications Read before ticking; understand what each box authorises
Terms of agreement (TOA) Specify what data is collected, how it is shared with partners, affiliates and advertisers, and retention and deletion policies. Users who don't read them may unknowingly consent Read and understand before accepting. In some cases users can negotiate terms or seek alternative agreements

2. Legal Issues in Data Use

2.1 Legislation

Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) — the primary Australian law governing how personal information is collected, used, stored and disclosed.

Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) — 13 principles governing:

1. The collection, use and disclosure of personal information
2. An organisation or agency's governance and accountability
3. Integrity and correction of personal information
4. The rights of individuals to access their personal information

Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme — requires entities to notify affected individuals and the OAIC about data breaches likely to result in serious harm.

2.2 Authorities Responsible for Data Protection

Authority Role
Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) The key authority. Enforces the Privacy Act, investigates complaints, and issues guidelines and resources for compliance

2.3 Data Sovereignty of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples

Indigenous data sovereignty is the right of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples to control the collection, ownership and application of data about their communities.

Consideration Requirement
Informed consent Data collection conducted with the community's informed consent, not just an individual's
Cultural respect Data handled in ways that respect cultural protocols and values
Control and ownership Communities empowered to manage and decide how their data is used and accessed

Confusing pair — ICIP vs Data sovereignty ICIP protects Indigenous cultural knowledge and heritage as intellectual property. Data sovereignty is the broader right to control data about the community itself — who collects it, who owns it, how it's used. ICIP is about the content. Sovereignty is about the control.

3. Social Issues — Curated and Communicated Data

3.1 The Core Distinction

Definition Effect
Curated data Data that has been selected, with context removed and the message simplified for impact Persuasive. Creates strong signals, encourages conformity
Communicated data Data presented with context and explanation — sample size, timeframe, limitations Informative. Meaning is clearer and less misleading

Curated data is not fake — it is selective. That distinction is examinable.

Worked example. A post reads: "80% of students say AI tools make learning easier."

CURATED (what was shared)      "80% of students say AI tools make learning easier"

COMMUNICATED (what was hidden)  Sample size: 50 students
                                Year conducted: 2 years ago
                                Limitations of the poll

The curated version moves data from informative to persuasive.

3.2 The Five Social Factors

Factor What it does
Data literacy Determines whether data informs or influences. Data-literate users question the source, notice missing context and recognise signal manipulation. Less literate users accept the signal at face value and change behaviour without questioning
Timeframes When data is shared changes how people react. Shared too late, people can't act. Outdated data exaggerates relevance, misguides decisions and creates false confidence
Signals impacting behaviour A figure like "80%" acts as a social signal suggesting what is normal, popular or acceptable. Signals influence behaviour even when data quality is weak
Data swamps Too much unorganised, undocumented or irrelevant data, making useful information hard to find. Systems trained on a data swamp repeat outdated assumptions, amplify weak signals and make poor recommendations
Educating users Teaching people how data works and how to interpret it helps them make smarter choices and protect their privacy

Confusing pair — Data warehouse vs Data swamp A data warehouse is a structured, documented central repository built for analysis. A data swamp is an unmanaged accumulation — no documentation, no clarity of purpose. Same volume, opposite governance.

4. Data Types and Levels of Measurement

4.1 Quantitative vs Qualitative

Definition Examples
Quantitative Numerical — can be counted or measured Scores, counts, dates, prices, temperatures
Qualitative Descriptive — categories, text, observations Comments, feedback, colours, categories, opinions

4.2 The Four Levels of Measurement

Level Definition Can you… Example
Nominal Named categories with no order Count only Eye colour, house, gender, suburb
Ordinal Categories with a meaningful order, but unequal gaps Rank Satisfaction (poor/fair/good), year level, medal placing
Interval Ordered, equal gaps, but no true zero Add and subtract Temperature in °C, calendar years, IQ
Ratio Ordered, equal gaps, true zero Add, subtract, multiply, divide Height, weight, price, age, count

The test for interval vs ratio: does zero mean "none of it"? 0 kg means no mass → ratio. 0 °C does not mean no temperature → interval. You cannot say "20 °C is twice as hot as 10 °C", but you can say "20 kg is twice 10 kg".

4.3 Structured, Unstructured and Alternative Data

Type Definition Examples
Structured Organised in a fixed format — rows, columns, fields Databases, spreadsheets, tables
Unstructured No fixed format, variable length Text, comments, images, video, memes
Alternative Non-traditional feedback signals from a different format or source Likes, emoticons, memes, click counts, sentiment labels

Alternative data as feedback:

  • Likes — indicate approval or support. The count gauges popularity and public response
  • Emoticons — pictorial representations of emotion. Provide insight into users' emotional responses, opinions and attitudes
  • Memes — images, video or text spread virally, often satirical. Popularity measures public interest and reveals opinion and attitudes

4.4 Informatics

Informatics is the study of the processing and management of information. It provides tools and techniques supporting the collection, organisation, analysis and interpretation of data.

Why it matters: without informatics, different data types remain disconnected and meaningless. Informatics enables a system to integrate, analyse and interpret diverse sources to produce informed outcomes.

On structured data On unstructured data
Organise into entities, attributes and keys Categorise or tag data
Apply queries — filtering, sorting, aggregation Convert into semi-structured or structured form
Support rule-based decisions (IF–THEN) Link user feedback to system rules or outcomes
Example: visitor counts, timestamps and locations analysed to identify peak times and trigger automated decisions Example: likes, emojis and comments interpreted as positive, neutral or negative and stored as sentiment values the system can reason with

5. Databases and SQL

5.1 Flat-file vs Relational

Flat-file Relational (RDBMS)
Structure A single table or file — plain text, CSV, JSON Multiple linked tables
Relationships None Tables linked by keys
Duplication High Reduced
Indexing None Yes
Best for Small, simple datasets, prototypes Enterprise-scale data, accuracy and organisation

5.2 Keys

Key Rules Purpose
Primary key Uniquely identifies each row · cannot be NULL · cannot be duplicated Ensures each record is unique; maintains data integrity
Foreign key Refers to a primary key in another table Links related data; creates the relationship between tables
Composite key Two or more fields together forming the primary key Used in join tables resolving many-to-many relationships

Example: Results.StudentID (foreign key) → Students.StudentID (primary key).

5.3 Relationships and Normalisation

Relationship Handling
One to many (1:∞) The most common. Each record in the primary key's table can have many matching records in the foreign key's table
One to one (1:1) Always possible — and usually preferable — to combine both tables into one
Many to many (∞:∞) Cannot be implemented directly. Must be resolved by creating a join table with two 1:∞ relationships, containing foreign keys to both original primary keys. Together these form a composite primary key

Normalisation improves a database's efficiency, consistency and accuracy, and makes it adaptable to changing enterprise needs.

Why redundancy is a problem: repeated data unnecessarily increases database size, and causes inconsistency during insert, delete and update operations.

Worked normalisation:

Problem Solution
Identical rows — can't tell orders apart Add an OrderID to uniquely identify each order
An Order column contains multiple values (non-atomic) Move the order items to a separate table
Customer Name is a composite value Break into First Name and Last Name columns
Repeated values for the same customer Move customer details to their own table, link by key

Data dictionary — documents the structure of the database: field names, data types, data format and other attributes. Serves as a reference ensuring consistency and shared understanding of the design.

5.4 SQL — LibreOffice Base

⚠️ Mixed-case names and names with spaces must be in double quotes: "YearLevel", "Student Name". Text values use single quotes: 'Blue'.

Basic SELECT

SELECT * FROM "Students";

SELECT "FirstName", "LastName", "YearLevel"
FROM "Students";

WHERE — filtering

SELECT * FROM "Students"
WHERE "YearLevel" = 12;

SELECT "FirstName", "LastName" FROM "Students"
WHERE "YearLevel" = 12 AND "House" = 'Blue';

WHERE "LastName" LIKE 'Sm%';        -- text matching, % is the wildcard

ORDER BY, DISTINCT, LIMIT

SELECT "StudentID", "Score" FROM "Results"
ORDER BY "Score" DESC;

SELECT DISTINCT "House" FROM "Students";

ORDER BY "Score" DESC LIMIT 5;                    -- may not work on all drivers
SELECT "StudentID", "Score" FETCH FIRST 5 ROWS ONLY;   -- fallback

Aggregates and GROUP BY

SELECT "Task", AVG("Score") AS "AvgScore"
FROM "Results"
GROUP BY "Task";

SELECT "YearLevel", COUNT(*) AS "NumStudents"
FROM "Students"
GROUP BY "YearLevel";

HAVING — filtering groups

SELECT "Task", AVG("Score") AS "AvgScore"
FROM "Results"
GROUP BY "Task"
HAVING AVG("Score") >= 70;

Confusing pair — WHERE vs HAVING WHERE filters individual rows, before grouping. HAVING filters groups, after GROUP BY has aggregated them. You cannot use an aggregate function in WHERE.

JOINs

-- INNER JOIN: only rows with matches in BOTH tables
SELECT s."FirstName", s."LastName", r."Task", r."Score"
FROM "Students" s
INNER JOIN "Results" r ON s."StudentID" = r."StudentID";

-- LEFT JOIN: ALL students, even those with no results
SELECT s."FirstName", s."LastName", r."Task", r."Score"
FROM "Students" s
LEFT JOIN "Results" r ON s."StudentID" = r."StudentID";

JOIN + WHERE + ORDER BY — the common report query

SELECT s."FirstName", s."LastName", r."Task", r."Score"
FROM "Students" s
INNER JOIN "Results" r ON s."StudentID" = r."StudentID"
WHERE s."YearLevel" = 12
ORDER BY r."Score" DESC;

JOIN + GROUP BY — averages per student

SELECT s."StudentID", s."FirstName", s."LastName",
       AVG(r."Score") AS "AvgScore"
FROM "Students" s
INNER JOIN "Results" r ON s."StudentID" = r."StudentID"
GROUP BY s."StudentID", s."FirstName", s."LastName";

Three-table JOIN

SELECT s."FirstName", s."LastName", c."ClassName", r."Task", r."Score"
FROM "Students" s
INNER JOIN "Classes" c ON s."ClassID" = c."ClassID"
INNER JOIN "Results" r ON s."StudentID" = r."StudentID"
ORDER BY c."ClassName", s."LastName";

INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE

INSERT INTO "MOVIE" ("MovieID", "Title", "Genre", "Rating")
VALUES (1, 'Inception', 'Sci-Fi', 'M');

UPDATE "MOVIE" SET "Rating" = 'MA15+' WHERE "MovieID" = 1;

DELETE FROM "MOVIE" WHERE "MovieID" = 1;

LibreOffice Base gotchas: query errors are usually quotes — use "FieldName" for mixed case or spaces. "Table not found" means the exact name doesn't match the Tables list. LIMIT may fail on some drivers, so use FETCH FIRST.

6. Spreadsheets and Dashboards

6.1 The Key Rule

One sheet = one table = one purpose. Even though spreadsheets are flat-file systems, multiple sheets let you separate data types, reduce repetition, improve clarity and support decision logic.

Data type How to store it
Structured — numeric or categorical, consistent format Directly in tables
Unstructured — text-based, descriptive, variable length As text fields, not mixed with numbers
Alternative — click counts, frequency totals, sentiment labels Often generated or summarised from other data

6.2 Linking Sheets Without SQL

Spreadsheets use logical links, not joins:

Matching IDs (e.g. RecordID)
VLOOKUP / XLOOKUP
FILTER
COUNTIF, AVERAGEIF

6.3 Summary Statistics

Function Returns
SUM Total of values
COUNT Number of data points
AVERAGE The mean — typical value
MIN / MAX The range

6.4 Analysis Features

Feature Use
Charts Transform numerical data into visual form so patterns, comparisons and trends are easier to see
Pivot tables Interactive summarising of large datasets without formulas. Drag-and-drop fields to build instant, filterable reports (e.g. total sales by region)
Slicers Interactive filter buttons for a pivot table or dashboard
Conditional formatting Automatically highlight cells meeting a condition
What-if modelling Change inputs to see the effect on outputs — scenario testing
Forms and reports Present data clearly for entry and for output

Chart selection:

Chart Shows
Scatter chart Relationships, trends and patterns between variables
Histogram Data distribution — detects skewness and outliers
Line graph Change over time

Filtering displays only specific parts of a dataset. It can lead to biased conclusions if too much data is excluded — that limitation is examinable.

7. Data Scale and Big Data

7.1 The Impact of Data Scale

Aspect The issue
Volume of raw data More records improve accuracy, but extreme size degrades system performance and increases cost
Storage Insufficient storage limits decision-making; storage choice affects speed and scalability; loss or corruption is catastrophic
Real-time and continuous streaming Delayed data can be useless. Data that never stops arriving creates technical challenges and drives system design
Opportunities for machine learning ML requires large datasets. Poor-quality data produces poor predictions
Changes in human behaviour Behaviour change increases volume and velocity, and modern data is increasingly unstructured
Ethical implications, incl. digital footprints Scale increases privacy risk and makes bias harder to detect

7.2 Big Data — The Three Vs

VOLUME    the amount of data
VARIETY   the range of types and sources
VELOCITY  the speed at which it arrives and must be processed

Data warehouse — a central repository storing large volumes of historical and current data from multiple sources.

Advantages Limitations
Supports complex queries · enables trend analysis · improves decision accuracy Complex to implement · requires skilled design · higher cost

8. Data Sampling and Storage

8.1 Sampling

Data sampling is selecting a subset of data from a larger dataset so decisions can be made efficiently without analysing everything.

Active collection Passive collection
Definition Collected intentionally from users Collected automatically, without direct user input
Examples Surveys, forms, questionnaires, user input fields System logs, sensor data, clickstream data, usage analytics
Strengths More control Large volume, real-time
Weaknesses Can introduce bias; depends on user participation Privacy concerns; less user awareness
Method Description Limitation
Manual A human selects and records data Time-consuming, error-prone
Computerised Software collects and samples automatically Bias if poorly designed

Poor sampling produces incorrect recommendations, biased outcomes and unreliable system behaviour. Effective sampling ensures decisions are representative, processing is efficient and the system scales.

8.2 Storage Methods

Method Advantages Limitations
Local storage Fast access · full control · no internet required Limited scalability · risk of data loss · unsuitable for large datasets or collaboration
Cloud storage Scalable · accessible anywhere · supports collaboration and backups Internet dependency · ongoing cost · privacy and compliance concerns
Portable storage media Portable · offline access · simple High risk of loss or theft · limited capacity · unsuitable for real-time systems
Data warehouses Supports complex queries · enables trend analysis · improves decision accuracy Complex · requires skilled design · higher cost

The judgements to make: local storage suits small-scale prototypes but limits scalability. Portable storage is generally unsuitable for a decision support system due to security and scalability risk. Cloud supports enterprise-scale systems. Data warehouses enable high-quality, enterprise-level decision support.

9. Errors, Uncertainty and Data Quality

9.1 The Three Problems

Definition
Errors Mistakes made in data collection, entry or processing — from minor typos to significant inaccuracies
Uncertainty Lack of precision or ambiguity, arising from the measurement process, the nature of the data, or the analysis method
Limitations Inherent constraints — sample size, coverage, representativeness, or the scope and reliability of the sources

9.2 Causes

Cause Effect
Data collection methods Self-reported data is subject to bias; observed data is subject to observer error
Sample size Small samples may not represent the population, producing high uncertainty
Data quality Poor accuracy or completeness produces errors and limitations
Data timeliness Outdated data doesn't reflect current trends, impacting validity

9.3 Raw vs Processed Data

Definition Risk
Raw data Not processed or analysed in any way More susceptible to collection and entry errors; often not in an analysable format
Processed data Cleaned, transformed or manipulated for analysis Processing reduces errors — but can introduce new uncertainty if methods are inappropriate or the data is manipulated unrepresentatively

Data bias — systematic errors arising from selective sampling, underrepresentation of groups, or measurement error. Affects the validity of conclusions and may perpetuate or amplify existing inequalities.

9.4 The Four Quality Criteria

Criterion The question
Relevance Is the data relevant to the purpose?
Accuracy Is it correct and error-free?
Validity Does it measure what it's supposed to?
Reliability Would you get the same results collecting it again?

9.5 Primary vs Secondary Data

Primary Secondary
Definition Collected directly by you for a specific purpose Collected by someone else, used by you
Examples Surveys, experiments, observations, interviews Books, journal articles, reports, government statistics
Relevance Designed to address your question directly — highly relevant May not match your question, but gives useful background
Accuracy Depends on careful collection; poor design produces poor data Harder to assess — you must trust the source
Validity Ensured by designing collection to measure what you want May not be valid, as it wasn't collected with your question in mind
Reliability Reliable if the method is well-structured and repeatable Reliable from credible sources using standardised, repeatable methods (e.g. ABS)

9.6 The Data Quality Chain

Data Collection (from different sources)
        ↓
Raw Data (unfiltered, uncleaned, incomplete)
        ↓
Data Processing (cleaning, sorting, filtering, summarising)
        ↓
Possible issues: errors · uncertainty · limitations · data bias
        ↓
Impact on quality: relevance · accuracy · validity · reliability
        ↓
Risk to decisions (incorrect or biased outputs)
        ↓
Need for data trust and verification  →  BLOCKCHAIN

10. Blockchain

Blockchain is a distributed ledger that manages and verifies data in a secure, transparent and decentralised manner. Instead of a central server, it works peer-to-peer, with nodes communicating directly across the network.

How it works:

  1. Transactions are verified and recorded as blocks
  2. Blocks are linked to form a chain
  3. Each block contains the hash of the previous block, so a previous block cannot be modified without invalidating every subsequent block
  4. Blocks are stored across multiple nodes, creating a decentralised ledger
  5. Transactions are confirmed by multiple participants, making falsification difficult

Four Applications

Application How blockchain is used
Online voting Creates a secure, transparent record of voting transactions, making voting tamper-proof
Online identities Digital identities stored as assets on the chain, authenticating individuals, organisations and devices. Offers greater security, privacy and control than traditional online identities
Tracking items of value Assets represented by unique identifiers called tokens, verifying ownership and tracking transfers. Uses public ledgers and smart contracts
Recordkeeping Maintaining and storing information in a secure, decentralised and tamper-proof manner

11. Data Mining, Machine Learning and Statistical Modelling

Definition
Data mining Discovering patterns, relationships and anomalies in large datasets
Machine learning Uses algorithms to identify patterns and relationships in large datasets, improving with more data
Statistical modelling Uses mathematical and statistical techniques to build models that make predictions

Used together to analyse and predict from large datasets — customer behaviour, market trends, sales data. The resulting models help organisations identify trends and make data-driven decisions.

Always state the limitation. Consider the biases that may exist in the data and the potential for errors in predictions. A model is only as good as the data behind it.

12. Confusing Terms — Quick Reference

Term Meaning Don't confuse with
Curated data Selected, context removed Communicated data — context included
Data warehouse Structured, documented repository Data swamp — unmanaged accumulation
Privacy The individual's right Confidentiality — the researcher's duty
ICIP Indigenous cultural knowledge as IP Data sovereignty — the right to control the data itself
Primary key Unique, not null, in this table Foreign key — refers to another table's primary key
WHERE Filters rows, before grouping HAVING — filters groups, after
INNER JOIN Only matching rows in both tables LEFT JOIN — all rows from the left table
Interval Equal gaps, no true zero Ratio — has a true zero
Nominal Categories, no order Ordinal — categories with order
Active collection User intentionally supplies it Passive — collected automatically
Raw data Unprocessed Processed — cleaned and transformed
Errors Mistakes Uncertainty — imprecision · Limitations — inherent constraints
Validity Measures what it should Reliability — repeatable results
Flat-file One table, no relationships Relational — multiple linked tables
Structured data Fixed format Unstructured — no format · Alternative — different source
